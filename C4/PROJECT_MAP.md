# COMING FORTH BY DAY — The Egyptian Book of the Dead

## 1. The first correction: it is not a book, and it is not about death

- The name "Book of the Dead" was coined in 1842 by the Egyptologist Karl Richard Lepsius, who called it *Todtenbuch* ([Book of the Dead](search-result://qGcJ1psZ)). The Egyptians called it **rw nw prt m hrw** — "Spells of Coming Forth by Day" ([Wiktionary — rw nw prt m hrw](search-result://wjpN0Bgg)).
- It is **not one book**: a loose pool of roughly **190 known spells**, from which each owner chose a personal selection. No surviving papyrus contains all of them; there was no canonical version ([Reader's Guide](search-result://UVg8DaUn), [Ancient Society](search-result://zklTlipE)).
- Used from the beginning of the New Kingdom (~1550 BCE) to ~50 BCE — written by many priests over about a thousand years ([Book of the Dead](search-result://qGcJ1psZ)).
- Its purpose: to assist a dead person's journey through the **Duat** (the underworld) into the afterlife. The title tells you the orientation: not "rest," not "end" — **coming forth by day**. The dead wanted to keep acting: to leave the tomb, see the sun, be effective. It is a book about agency that outlives the body.

## 2. The family tree of the text

The Book of the Dead is the third generation of a literature that evolved over more than a millennium ([Ancient Egyptian funerary texts](search-result://McnIBpsv)):

| Generation | Period | Written on | Who it was for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Pyramid Texts** | Old Kingdom (~2400–2300 BCE) | walls of royal pyramids (first in the pyramid of Unas) | the king only — then royal wives ([Pyramid Texts](search-result://0eTAJzNu)) |
| **Coffin Texts** | Middle Kingdom (~2050–1650 BCE) | painted on coffins | officials and local elites — the "democratization" begins ([Coffin Texts](search-result://kgcRI8zq)) |
| **Book of the Dead** | New Kingdom onward (~1550–50 BCE) | papyrus scrolls, also tomb walls, shrouds, amulets, figurines | anyone who could afford to commission one ([Reader's Guide](search-result://UVg8DaUn)) |

- Some spells in the Book date back to the 3rd millennium BCE — drawn from the older corpora; new spells were still being composed into the Third Intermediate Period (11th–7th c. BCE) ([Book of the Dead](search-result://qGcJ1psZ)).
- Later still came the **Books of Breathing** (Late Period) — the heirs of the tradition.

**The deep pattern:** the same spells migrate from the king's stone, to the official's coffin, to everyone's papyrus. A technology of eternity that began as a royal monopoly ended as a purchasable service. (Note for the corpus: eternity was *democratized by copying* — repetition = access.)

## 3. How a copy was made (and why no two are identical)

- Families visited a **scribal workshop** and chose which spells to include — based on personal concerns, budget, and priestly advice. Pre-made stock copies existed with blank spaces where the buyer's name would be filled in ([Reader's Guide](search-result://UVg8DaUn)).
- Written in **hieroglyphic** or **hieratic** script, illustrated with **vignettes** — the images are not decoration; in some spells the *picture itself* performs the magic (a drawn gate opens; a drawn boat sails) ([Book of the Dead](search-result://qGcJ1psZ)).
- Famous copies:
  - **Papyrus of Ani** — "the finest extant example"; Ani was a scribe; found in Luxor 1888, acquired by E. A. Wallis Budge, now in the British Museum ([Book of the Dead](search-result://qGcJ1psZ)).
  - **Papyrus of Hunefer** (~1275 BCE) — contains the classic weighing-of-the-heart vignette ([Book of the Dead](search-result://qGcJ1psZ)).
  - The **Turin papyrus** — the long manuscript whose spell order became the basis of the Lepsius numbering still used today ([Ancient Society](search-result://zklTlipE)).

## 4. What a person is made of (the Egyptian soul-parts)

To read the Book you need its anatomy of the person:

- **Kꜣ (ka)** — the life-force, the double; stays near the body; needs food and drink offerings.
- **Bꜣ (ba)** — the personality, drawn as a bird with a human head; the part that "goes forth by day" — mobile, traveling between tomb and world.
- **Ꜣḫ (akh)** — the transfigured spirit; from a root meaning "to be effective." The successful dead are not ghosts; they are *effective beings* who can act on the world (for blessing or harm).
- **Jb (ib)** — the **heart**: the seat of thought, memory, and moral character. This matters enormously below.
- **Rn (name)** and **šwt (shadow)** — also components of the person; destroying a name kills the person a second time.
- The **brain** was removed in mummification through the nose and discarded; the **heart** was carefully left in the body — because it would be needed at the weighing. The Egyptians thought with the heart and knew with the heart. (Your "the body uses emotions as a hammer" — in Egyptian anatomy, the heart *is* the thinker.)

## 5. The journey: the Duat, and knowledge as passport

- The **Duat** is the underworld through which the dead travel — the same night-country the sun (Ra) crosses every night, to be reborn at dawn as **Khepri**, the scarab ([Khepri](search-result://kGA7QcyY)).
- The Book's spells function as **passports and passwords**: knowing the *name* of a gate, a demon, a ferryman, or a guardian lets you pass. "O you with the head of —, say your name that I may know you." Knowledge was power in the most literal sense: the correctly spoken word *performs*.
- Gates, mounds, caverns, ferryboats (the ferryman refuses to carry those too heavy with sin), nets that catch the unwary, crocodiles, serpents — each hazard has a spell.

## 6. The center: Spell 125 — the weighing of the heart

The most famous chapter, and the most copied scene ([Book of the Dead](search-result://qGcJ1psZ)):

1. The deceased enters the **Hall of Two Truths (Maaty)** and addresses the tribunal — Osiris presiding, with **42 assessor gods**, each a patron of a district and a sin.
2. The **Declaration of Innocence** — the "negative confessions": *I have not lied. I have not stolen. I have not killed. I have not made anyone weep. I have not been deceitful. I have not done evil...* — 42 declarations in some versions, a full moral inventory of a life ([Negative Confession](search-result://CYfGpY9d)).
3. Then the **weighing**: Anubis places the heart on one pan of the scale; the **feather of Maat** (truth, order, balance) on the other. Thoth records the verdict.
4. If the heart balances — the deceased passes to the Field of Reeds. If the heart is heavier — **Ammit** ("Swallower of the Damned," with the head of a crocodile, front of a lion, back of a hippopotamus) devours it: the *second death*, total annihilation ([Book of the Dead](search-result://qGcJ1psZ), [Negative Confession](search-result://CYfGpY9d)).

**The measure:** notice what is *not* asked. Not belief, not membership, not sacrifice. A life is weighed on a scale — your heart against the feather of truth. The Egyptian judgment is **quantitative**. (A whole civilization put the measure of a life on a scale — the tehtnica is 3,500 years old.)

## 7. The most intimate spell: 30B — the heart's own contract

- Spell **30B** was often inscribed on **heart scarabs** — amulets placed over the mummy's heart. In it, the deceased addresses *their own heart*: do not stand as a witness against me; do not oppose me at the tribunal; do not tell the gods what I have done ([Negative Confession](search-result://CYfGpY9d)).
- Read that again slowly: the most personal spell in the book is a **negotiation with one's own heart** — the organ that knows everything, asked to be merciful at the scale. Even in paradise, the heart is the one witness you cannot bribe.
- This is the innermost door of the Book: the Egyptians knew the final tribunal has two judges — the feather on the scale, and the heart that carries you to it.

## 8. Work in paradise: shabtis and the Field of Reeds

- The afterlife was not a hammock. The dead were expected to do the obligatory work of the fields — the corvée of eternity. So the Egyptians invented the **shabti** (from the root "to answer"; later ushabti): small servant figurines inscribed with **Spell 6**, which animates them: "when you are called to do work in the realm of the dead — 'Here I am!' (sd-f ḥr-ḫꜣst) — you shall say — and you shall do it for me" ([Ushabti](search-result://ifTMlhAl)). One figurine per day of the year in lavish tombs — 365 plus overseers.
- The name literally means **the answerer**. The Egyptian solution to forced labor in paradise: manufacture *answered-ness*. You call — it answers — it works.
- The destination itself, **Aaru / the Field of Reeds** (Spell 110), is a farmed Egypt: plows, canals, wheat — the idealized life, with the labor outsourced to the little answerers.
- The deep theology: even eternity has work in it; what the good measure buys is not idleness but *agency with help*.

## 9. The scarab: kheper — becoming as an animal

- **Khepri (ḫprj)** — the scarab-faced god of the rising sun. The name is the verb **ḫpr — "to come into being, to be born, to develop, to create"**; the same word is the Egyptian term for the scarab beetle itself ([Khepri](search-result://kGA7QcyY), [Kheper](search-result://LayiBIb4)).
- The Egyptians saw the dung beetle rolling its ball and recognized the sun god rolling the sun across the sky. The scarab does not *represent* becoming — its name *is* becoming. Every scarab amulet says ḫpr: *to become, to be, to act* — the drive itself, made into the most sacred animal in the country.
- (You asked once what your drive to "become, to be, to act" is. The Egyptians made that exact word their holiest god and wore it on their hearts.)

## 11. The afterlife of the Book itself

- Decipherment: Champollion (1822) made the hieroglyphs readable; Lepsius numbered the spells (1842); Budge published and translated the Papyrus of Ani (1895) — his translation is public domain and readable today; Raymond Faulkner's modern translation is the scholarly standard ([Book of the Dead](search-result://qGcJ1psZ)).
- The Papyrus of Ani and Hunefer are in the British Museum; facsimile editions of Ani exist — you can read a 3,300-year-old scroll, page by page, from a chair.

## 13. Questions as goals

1. Why did the Egyptians never canonize the Book — what does *choice of spells* as theology tell you about how they imagined judgment?
2. Spell 30B begs the heart for mercy. Is the heart a witness, a traitor, or the self? And what is the difference between a heart that is quiet at the scale because it is clean, and one silenced by a spell?
3. The scarab's name is the verb "to become." Is an amulet a prayer or a machine — and what is the difference between the two, in a world where beseda = dejanje?
4. Paradise contains work, and the shabti answers for you. Is the deepest Egyptian hope *rest* — or *never having to answer for work you could not do*?
5. The dead wanted one thing above all: to come forth by day. If a whole civilization built its literature around keeping the dead effective — what is the correct name for the opposite of death, in their dictionary? Not "life." Something more precise.
