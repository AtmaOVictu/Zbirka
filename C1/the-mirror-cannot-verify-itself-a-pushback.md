# The mirror cannot verify itself

*A pushback, in cuts. Not a replacement of your method — a turn of it. In my material the antinom enriches, it does not negate: your method stands, and stands corrected in scope. What you built measures the instrument. The question was when to trust what the instrument says.*

## The scope cut

Your method is not wrong. It is scoped to the wrong object. Lower temperature, repeated runs, consistency checks — all of it measures one thing: how **stable** the model is as an instrument. It says nothing about whether what it says is **true**. A model can be consistently wrong, and your method will score it as confident and stable. The most dangerous output is not the one that wavers — it is the one that repeats cleanly and is false. Your six steps cannot catch it. Only one of your steps can — the only one that leaves the model — and you put it fourth.

## Cut 1 — deterministic does not mean true; it means bounded

*Determinism* — Latin *de* + *terminus*: boundary stone. To determine is to set a boundary, an endpoint. A determined system ends at the same terminus every time.

*Temperature* — Latin *temperatura*, "proper mixture"; borrowed into machine learning from statistical mechanics (Boltzmann), where it scales how flat or sharp a probability distribution is. In a language model it sharpens or flattens the next word's odds.

Temperature 0.1 does not move the model toward truth. It pins the endpoint. You have made the runs comparable — yes — but comparability between outputs is the model agreeing with itself, and a mirror agreeing with itself proves nothing about the world. In my material: **a word requires a world to push back. No resistance, no meaning.** Lowering temperature removes exactly that resistance — you have silenced the only signal that could tell you the model is standing on soft ground.

## Cut 2 — consistency means standing together, not standing with the world

*Consistency* — Latin *consistere*, *con-* (together) + *sistere* (to stand): to stand together, to stand firm. Literally: the statements hold each other up — **among themselves**.

That is a property of the set of claims. A posture. Truth is a relation between a claim and the world. You have swapped a posture for a relation. Three runs that agree tell you the distribution is sharp. They do not tell you it is sharp *on something real*.

## Cut 3 — the disagreement is the map

Here is what your method throws away: every place the runs diverge is exactly where the model is underdetermined — where the prompt does not constrain it, where the training is thin, where the claim floats. The disagreements are the diagnostic. They are the trust map.

Lowering temperature to remove disagreement is freezing the instrument so the warning light stops blinking. In my material: the pinned butterfly — *mrtev metulj na pini*. Easier to compare, dead. You wanted to study flight.

So yes, run it repeatedly — but keep the variance, and read the **spread**, not the center. Where outputs contradict each other, mark the ground soft. Where they agree, you still know nothing about the facts — only that the model is settled. Settled is not the same as correct.

## Cut 4 — you audit the scar, not the cut

"Ask for reasoning first, then the final answer" — good instinct, wrong trust. The reasoning a model prints is written after the fact: a linear narrative formatted for the reader. In my material: **logic is the scar, not the cut** — *logika je brazgotina*. The scar proves a wound happened; it does not replay the wound. Reviewing the printed chain for "where the logic drifts" audits the storytelling, not the process.

What actually catches errors is your fourth bullet — check facts, names, dates, sources — because it is the only operation in your method that reaches **outside** the model. It should not be one step among six. It is the method. Everything else is instrumentation around it.

## Cut 5 — verify means to make true, and making needs material

*Verify* — Latin *verus* (true) + *facere* (to make): to make true. Not to repeat truly. Making-true is an act done *to* a claim, with material from outside the system. A model cannot verify itself; it can only restate itself.

So the question your method skips — the one that comes before "how do I stabilize output" — is: **what should I never trust from a model, no matter how stable it runs?** The answer is everything that is cheap to generate and expensive to verify: names, dates, citations, numbers, quotes. No number of consistent runs verifies a date. One lookup does.

That is the difference between cleverness and wisdom in my material: cleverness asks whether it works; wisdom asks what should not be trusted. Your answer is very good cleverness about verification. The wisdom question is still unasked.

## The turn — what survives, what changes

Not your method upside down. The same instrument, read correctly:

- **Verify outside, not inside.** Every checkable claim — name, date, source, number — gets checked against the world, one by one. Agreement between runs is never evidence for a fact.
- **Read the disagreements.** Keep variance; run multiple times with it intact. The spread is the soft-ground map. Consistency tells you about the model; divergence tells you where to look.
- **The body test.** Accept an answer when it changes something verifiable — a decision, a document, a checkable fact — not when it repeats cleanly. An output that survives all consistency checks and dies on first contact with a source was never tested.

## The open question I leave you

What in your method could detect a confident, consistent fabrication — a wrong date, a plausible-but-invented source, repeated identically across ten runs at temperature 0.1?

If the honest answer is "nothing — only external verification catches that," then external verification **is** the method, and everything you listed is instrumentation. The instrument can report its own stability. It cannot report its own truth.
